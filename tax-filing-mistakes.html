Common Tax Filing Mistakes

How to Avoid Costly Errors When Filing Your Taxes

Published: January 2026 | 7 min read | Tax Education

Filing taxes accurately is crucial for avoiding penalties, audits, and unnecessary stress. Even small mistakes can lead to delays in processing your return, reduced refunds, or additional taxes owed. By understanding common tax filing mistakes, you can take steps to avoid them and ensure your tax return is filed correctly.

Mathematical Errors

One of the most common mistakes is simple mathematical errors. Adding or subtracting incorrectly can lead to significant discrepancies in your tax liability. The IRS will typically correct mathematical errors, but this can delay processing and may affect other calculations on your return.

How to avoid: Use tax preparation software that automatically performs calculations, double-check all entries, or have a tax professional review your return before filing.

Incorrect or Missing Social Security Numbers

Social Security numbers must be accurate for every person listed on your tax return. Incorrect SSNs can delay processing and may cause the IRS to reject your return entirely. This is particularly important for dependents, as their SSNs are required to claim them.

How to avoid: Carefully copy SSNs from Social Security cards, verify that all SSNs are entered correctly, and ensure you have valid SSNs for all dependents you claim.

Misspelled Names

The names on your tax return must match the names on Social Security records exactly. Misspelled names can cause processing delays and may prevent the IRS from matching your return to your records.

How to avoid: Use the names exactly as they appear on Social Security cards. If you've recently changed your name due to marriage or other reasons, ensure you've updated your records with the Social Security Administration.

Filing Status Errors

Choosing the wrong filing status can significantly impact your tax liability. Some people incorrectly file as "Head of Household" when they don't meet the requirements, or married couples may not choose the most advantageous filing status.

How to avoid: Understand the requirements for each filing status and choose the one that applies to your situation. If you're married, calculate your tax under both "Married Filing Jointly" and "Married Filing Separately" to determine which is more beneficial.

Math Errors in Credits and Deductions

Miscalculating tax credits and deductions is another common error. This includes incorrectly calculating the Child Tax Credit, Earned Income Tax Credit, or deduction amounts. These errors can result in either overpaying or underpaying taxes.

How to avoid: Use the IRS worksheets provided in tax instructions, utilize tax software with built-in calculations, or work with a tax professional who understands complex credit and deduction rules.

Forgetting to Sign and Date the Return

An unsigned tax return is considered invalid and will be rejected by the IRS. This simple oversight can delay processing and may result in penalties if the deadline has passed.

How to avoid: Make signing and dating your return one of the last steps before filing. If filing electronically, use your PIN or electronic signature as required by the software.

Incorrect Bank Account Information

If you're requesting a direct deposit refund or want to pay electronically, providing incorrect bank account information can cause significant problems. Your refund may be delayed, or your payment may be rejected.

How to avoid: Double-check bank account numbers and routing numbers against your checks or bank statements before submitting your return.

Not Reporting All Income

Failing to report all income is a serious mistake that can lead to penalties, interest, and potential audits. The IRS receives copies of most income documents (W-2s, 1099s) and will know if you haven't reported income.

How to avoid: Gather all income documents before filing, including W-2s, 1099s, and records of any other income. Report all income, even if you didn't receive a form for it.

Claiming Ineligible Dependents

Claiming someone as a dependent when they don't meet the requirements is a common error. This can trigger audits and require you to repay credits and deductions plus penalties and interest.

How to avoid: Understand the dependency tests (relationship, age, residency, support, and joint return tests) before claiming anyone as a dependent. The IRS provides an interactive tool to help determine dependency status.

Not Filing Because You Can't Pay

Some taxpayers don't file because they owe taxes they can't pay. This is a mistake that leads to additional penalties. The failure-to-file penalty is typically much higher than the failure-to-pay penalty.

How to avoid: Always file your return on time, even if you can't pay. The IRS offers payment plans and other options for taxpayers who can't pay in full. File your return and explore payment options.

Missing Deadlines

Missing tax filing deadlines results in penalties and interest charges. Even if you're due a refund, filing late can delay your refund and may cause you to lose the refund if you wait too long (typically three years).

How to avoid: Mark tax deadlines on your calendar, start preparing your return early, and consider requesting an extension if needed. Remember that extensions only extend filing time, not payment time.

Not Keeping Copies or Records

Failing to keep copies of your tax return and supporting documents can create problems if you're audited or need to reference previous returns. The IRS recommends keeping records for at least three years.

How to avoid: Save electronic and paper copies of your tax return and all supporting documents. Organize them by tax year and keep them in a secure location.

Business-Specific Mistakes

Business owners face additional potential mistakes:

  • Mixing personal and business expenses: Failing to properly separate personal and business finances can lead to disallowed deductions and audit risks.
  • Not paying estimated taxes: Business owners who don't make quarterly estimated payments may face underpayment penalties.
  • Incorrect classification of workers: Misclassifying employees as independent contractors can result in significant penalties.
  • Missing payroll tax filings: Failing to file required payroll tax returns can lead to substantial penalties.

Pennsylvania-Specific Mistakes

Pennsylvania taxpayers should also be aware of state-specific errors:

  • Not filing local Earned Income Tax: Pennsylvania residents must file local EIT returns in addition to state and federal returns.
  • Incorrect residency status: Part-year residents must correctly allocate income between Pennsylvania and other states.
  • Missing Pennsylvania-specific credits: Pennsylvania offers various tax credits that taxpayers may overlook.

How to Avoid Mistakes

  • Start early: Begin preparing your tax return well before the deadline to avoid rushing.
  • Use tax software: Quality tax software reduces errors by performing calculations and checking for common mistakes.
  • Organize documents: Keep all tax documents organized throughout the year.
  • Review carefully: Take time to review your return thoroughly before filing.
  • Seek professional help: Consider working with a tax professional, especially for complex situations.
  • E-file: Electronic filing reduces errors caused by manual data entry and speeds up processing.

What to Do If You Make a Mistake

If you discover an error after filing, don't panic. You can file an amended return using Form 1040-X for federal taxes and the appropriate form for Pennsylvania. Generally, you have three years from the original filing deadline to file an amended return to claim a refund, but there's no time limit to correct errors that result in additional tax owed (though penalties and interest will accrue).

Avoiding tax filing mistakes requires attention to detail, organization, and understanding of tax requirements. This guide provides general information about common tax filing mistakes.
